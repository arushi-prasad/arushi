DesignOps / 01

Where the work actually went

Assigning work to designers is the easy half. The harder half is holding priorities, capacity, skills and delivery dates in one view, so a decision about one is visibly a decision about the rest. Below are the three artefacts I used to do that, rendered from the sheets themselves: 77 work items, 20 people across 38 week columns, and two quarters of objectives.

Snapshots from 2024, not live status. Where the source cell was empty you will see a dash, and where the sheets record a target rather than an outcome the page says so. Names are replaced with role labels throughout.

02 · The model

How the artefacts connect

A synthesis, not a diagram copied from the source. The sheets document the stages individually; the sequence below is how I read their relationship to one another.

  1. Stage 01

    Business & product priorities

    What the organisation has decided matters this quarter. Everything downstream is a negotiation against this, not a replacement for it.

  2. Stage 02

    Quarterly objectives & key results

    Themes become objectives, objectives become named initiatives with a measure or a milestone attached. Documented per quarter and per person.

  3. Stage 03

    Work intake & prioritisation

    Each incoming item is recorded with squad, requirement type, complexity and a P0–P5 priority before anyone is assigned to it.

  4. Stage 04

    Designer & squad allocation

    UX and UI owners assigned separately, against the availability tracker rather than against an assumption about who is free.

  5. Stage 05

    Delivery tracking & reviews

    Status is held per design phase, so “in progress” says which phase. Reviews sit against the same record.

  6. Stage 06

    Learning, replanning, next cycle

    What the quarter taught goes back into the next objective set and the next allocation round.

Feeds back into stages 02 & 03. The model is a cycle, not a line

03 · Work allocation

The allocation sheet

One row per work item: who owns it, which squad it belongs to, which product manager it pairs with, what stage it has reached and what it was due. Filter it by squad, discipline or status. This is the artefact, not a picture of it.

How complete it really was

A planning artefact is only as good as the discipline around filling it in, and this one was kept up unevenly. Worth showing rather than hiding: it is the thing that made me redesign the sheet.

04 · The instrument

The template behind it

The snapshot above runs on seven columns. The template it was meant to run on asks for fifteen fields, tracks a story through five design stages rather than one status, and constrains seven of those fields to a dropdown so two people filling a row choose the same words. The gap between the two is the real story of the quarter.

Priority and complexity are in the template and in its one worked row, but no row in the July snapshot carries either. So there is no priority-versus-complexity plot on this page: the scheme existed, the data to plot it does not.

08 · What it enabled

And what it did not

What a system like this is built to give you, stated as intent rather than as measured gain. None of these workbooks records a before-and-after, so this page claims none.

  1. 01

    Visibility

    One place where every open design item has an owner, a squad and a stage. The filters above are the daily use: pull up a squad, see its work.

  2. 02

    A shared vocabulary

    Seven constrained fields instead of free text, so “complexity” and “priority” mean the same thing in two squads. The 21 different spellings of status in the snapshot show exactly what happens without it.

  3. 03

    Availability, not capacity

    The tracker makes absence visible a week ahead, which is enough to reassign work before it slips. It is not a capacity model, and treating it as one would have been the mistake.

  4. 04

    Alignment that survives contact

    Themes down to individual commitments, each with a target, so a reallocation can be argued against something written down.

  5. 05

    The honest gap

    No estimates, no capacity denominator and no recorded outcomes. So this system could tell me who was free and what was late, and could not tell me whether the team was over-committed. That is what I would build next.

09 · Provenance

Where this came from

Every table, grid and matrix above is generated from three private workbooks by a build step, so nothing on this page was typed in by hand. What that step strips before anything reaches the page:

  • All 24 designer and PM names → role labels
  • Leave dates → a three-state weekly signal
  • The levelling and org-banding table → excluded
  • Internal links → none carried over

The workbooks themselves are not published and are not in this repository. Two of them describe different revisions of the same objective framework and are shown as two, never merged into one. Dates are the targets the sheets held; no source records what shipped, so no outcome is claimed anywhere on this page.
