01 · Project Overview

A claim is one object, and nobody involved can see all of it.

A health claim management system is the machinery between a hospital bill and a bank transfer. A member raises a claim, names an ailment, attaches documents and waits. An adjuster receives those documents, reconciles them against a policy, decides what is covered and produces a figure. Both of those are the same claim. They happen in two different interfaces that share no view of it.

I took the member portal I use myself as the starting evidence, followed one claim across the handoff, and redesigned the adjuster side as a four stage workflow where every figure carries the clause it was decided under.

What the system does, and who is in it

The member side handles intimation, hospital selection, document submission and claim history, across cashless and reimbursement routes. The adjuster side handles everything after the handoff: establishing that a document set is complete, tying each document to the policy clause it evidences, deciding each bill line, and producing a settlement figure that has to survive being questioned months later.

Four parties touch a claim. The member files it. The adjuster decides it. The hospital billing desk needs to know whether the stay is cashless, and the employer who owns the policy sets the terms every deduction is taken under. Only the first two have an interface. Section 04 sets out what each needs and which of them this project could actually observe.

My role

Design Leader. I set the framing, ran the analysis of the existing portal, defined the experience architecture, designed the claim workflow and built it as a working demo so the argument could be tested rather than described.

What that covered, in order: capturing and reading the live member portal; establishing what the source mockup actually contained; modelling the claim across the handoff; designing three stages that did not previously exist; specifying the system states a real claim spends its time in; and writing the validation this work still needs before any of it should be built.

02 · The Existing Experience

What the member portal does today, screen by screen.

Eight captures of the live Vidal Health member portal, taken on 29 October 2026 from my own account. This is a production product I did not design, and it does a great deal correctly: the policy is legible at a glance, the claim routes are all reachable from one place, and the escalation path is named, which most portals do not bother to do. What follows are design observations from the visible interface, not findings about users.

These screenshots are cropped to remove the browser and desktop, and redacted where they carried a member ID, a date of birth, two mobile numbers, an email address, and the name, work email and mobile number of a Vidal employee listed on the escalation panel. The redaction bars are deliberate and labelled. Original files are untouched.

Claim initiation

Intimate Your Claim is where a claim becomes a record. Two panels: eight fields of claim detail, and a hospital to attach to it.

Eight fields in a four by two grid, seven of them required. Alternate Mobile Number is the only optional one. Ailment is free text. Estimated Amount is asked for in rupees with no basis offered and no indication of what it affects. Likely Date of Admission and Expected Discharge Date carry no stated relationship to each other.

Below it, Hospital Details reduces to a single action. The hint under it reads Hospital name, phone number, address, pincode, which describes four ways to find a hospital.

Nothing on this screen is wrong. It collects what the process needs. What it does not do is tell the member which of these answers will shape the outcome, or what happens next.

1

Ailment is free text. "Bone Surgery" is what was typed. The clause that decides the claim depends on the procedure, so a free text field here pushes the classification work downstream to the adjuster.

2

Both dates are the same day. The form accepts an admission and a discharge on 31 October with no warning. A same day stay fails the twenty-four hour hospitalisation condition in most policies, which is a decidable fact at the point of entry.

3

Estimated Amount, unanchored. Two hundred thousand rupees, exactly half the sum insured shown on the dashboard, though nothing on this screen says so. It is entered with no reference to the balance, the room rent eligibility or the co-payment, and it will not resemble the settlement.

Hospital search

The search opens as a panel over the claim form, which stays filled behind it. Keeping the member in the claim rather than sending them to a separate destination is the right pattern. What the panel returns is the problem.

1

State and city are required. Hospital name is not. The asterisks are on the two administrative fields. A member who knows the hospital but not the state it files under cannot run the search, and the four ways of finding a hospital promised on the previous screen (name, phone, address, pincode) reduce here to name plus two dropdowns.

2

Three hospitals found. One hospital name, one city, three entries, plus an Add New Hospital action if none of them is right.

3

Registry identifiers, not relationships. The results are separated by a Rohini number and an empanelment number. Notably, the first result in a Pune search carries empanelment number HOS-MUM-77. Neither identifier answers the only question the member has: is this hospital in network for my policy, and is cashless available here.

4

The claim stays behind the panel. The form is dimmed rather than discarded, so selecting a hospital returns the member to a claim they have already part-filled. This is the part of the flow that works.

Claim details and tracking

Claim History is a separate destination from Intimation and Claim Submission, so a claim in progress is read by going to a different screen from the one it was filed on. The member record carries the other half of the support experience.

Every statement in this section is an observation about what is on screen. None of it is a measured usability finding, because no usability work was run. A reader should treat these as the hypotheses that shaped the design, and section 11 as the test that would confirm or kill them.

03 · The Problem

Seven problems, one failure.

It would be easy to list what is hard about filing a health claim and call that a problem statement. It would also be wrong, because these are not seven independent usability issues. They are one failure expressed seven times: at no point does the system assemble a single view of the claim, so every interface asks someone to act on a fragment of it.

The chain below is how that compounds. Each link opens with the state the previous one left the claim in.

Link 01 · before the form

Everything a member must already know before a claim form can help them.

Which route appliesCashless, reimbursement or hospital search sit side by side as equals. The right one depends on facts answered elsewhere.
What the policy coversThe dashboard says Hospitalisation Only and gives a balance. Room rent eligibility, co-payment and sub-limits are what decide the settlement.
Whether the hospital is in networkThe search that answers this is a sibling of Claims in the navigation, not a step inside it.

Which leaves the claim: started on a route the member chose before they could find out whether it was the right one.

Link 02 · in the form

A form that collects exactly what the process needs, and gives nothing back.

Eight fields, seven requiredIncluding a mobile number and an email the account already holds, and an alternate mobile number that is the one optional field.
Ailment as free textThe clause that decides the claim depends on the procedure. Typing it as prose defers the classification to someone downstream.
An estimate with no anchorA rupee figure requested with no reference to the balance, the room cap or the co-payment, and no statement of what it affects.
A search that returns registry numbersRohini and empanelment identifiers, where the member needs network status for this policy.

Which leaves the claim: recorded, with a classification nobody has made and a number nobody has checked against the policy it will be decided under.

Link 03 · after the handoff

The window where a claim is most decidable is the window where it is least visible.

Status without stateClaim History is a separate destination from the screens the claim was filed on, and it reports a stage rather than what that stage is waiting for.
No next stepNothing tells the member what the claim needs from them, or what would release it.
Reasons produced, then discardedThe adjuster establishes, clause by clause, exactly why each amount is what it is. None of that reasoning is kept with the figure.

Which leaves the member: with a settled amount, a remembered estimate, and no account of the distance between them. That distance is what people mean when they say a claim went wrong.

The handoff, drawn

The same claim, as each side meets it. Neither lane can see the other, and the row between them is where the continuity is lost.

The claim, as the member meets it

Choose a routeCashless, reimbursement, or find a hospital.
Intimate the claimEight fields, including an estimated amount.
Find the hospitalName, state and city, then pick from the results.
Submit documentsThen wait.

The handoff. Neither side sees the other's view of the same claim.

The claim, as the adjuster meets it

A pile of documentsOf unknown completeness.
A policyWith clauses that have to be applied line by line.
A decisionThat has to be defensible months later.
A figureThe member will compare against their estimate.

So the problem to solve is not a better claim form. It is a shared view of one claim: the same documents, the same clauses and the same arithmetic, visible to the person filing and the person deciding. This project takes the adjuster half of that, because it is where the reasons are produced and currently thrown away.

05 · Key Findings & Opportunities

Six themes, three of which decide the rest.

Each theme is read the same way: what was difficult, why that mattered, and where the opening is. Three are marked primary, because a findings section in which everything is equally loud has not prioritised anything. Those three are the ones that make the other three expensive.

01 Primary Observed

Information hierarchy and discoverability

What was difficult

The facts that decide a claim are distributed across screens by subject rather than by decision. Network status is a sibling of Claims. Cover terms are a two-word summary on the dashboard. A claim in progress is on a third screen from the one it was filed on.

Why it mattered

Every one of the other five themes is a consequence of this. A route is chosen badly because the deciding fact is elsewhere; an estimate is unanchored because the balance is elsewhere; a status is opaque because the reason is elsewhere.

The opportunity

Organise around the claim rather than around the subject. One claim, one place, carrying its documents, its clauses and its arithmetic together.

02 Observed

Claim initiation and navigation

What was difficult

Three equally weighted routes, where the correct one depends on whether the hospital is in network and whether the member is before or after admission. One level down, reimbursement splits again into three destinations that are really three stages of one claim.

Why it mattered

A wrong route is not a wrong click. Picking reimbursement when cashless was available means the member pays the hospital in full and waits, which is the most expensive navigation error in the product.

The opportunity

Ask the two questions that determine the route, where and when, and let the system name the route, rather than presenting the routes and asking the member to self-select.

03 Observed

Form usability and data entry

What was difficult

Eight fields, seven required, in one flat grid. Mobile number and email are asked for on a screen reached from an authenticated account. Ailment is free text. Two dates are collected with no stated relationship to each other.

Why it mattered

Each field is cheap on its own. Together they make a form that cannot validate what it collects: a same-day admission and discharge passes, and a free text ailment cannot be checked against any clause.

The opportunity

Drop what the account already holds, structure the one field that decides the clause, and validate the pair of dates against the condition that actually governs them.

04 Observed

Hospital search and selection

What was difficult

State and city are required; the hospital name is not. One name in one city returns three entries, separated by a Rohini number and an empanelment number, one of which, in a Pune search, is coded for Mumbai.

Why it mattered

The search surfaces the operational identity of a hospital and withholds its relationship to the member's cover. That relationship is the only part the member can act on, and the one that decides which route they should have taken.

The opportunity

Lead each result with network status for this policy, then the address a person would recognise, then the registry number. Make the administrative fields optional filters rather than a gate.

05 Primary Observed

Policy and coverage visibility

What was difficult

The policy is summarised as Hospitalisation Only with a balance of ₹4,00,000. Room rent eligibility, co-payment, sub-limits and the non-payable list, the four things that produce almost every deduction, are not on the screen where a claim is filed.

Why it mattered

A room rent cap does not only cap the room. In most policies it prorates the surgeon, theatre and anaesthetist charges with it. That is the single largest deduction on a typical claim and the one members are least likely to have anticipated.

The opportunity

Put clauses where they bite rather than in a reference document: attached to the line they govern, in words, at the moment the figure changes.

06 Primary Observed

Claim status and progress transparency

What was difficult

After submission there are four consecutive stages (document review, policy mapping, coverage decision, settlement) in which the member sees nothing. Claim History reports that a claim is in progress. It does not report what the claim is waiting for.

Why it mattered

Those four stages are exactly where the reasons are produced. The adjuster establishes, clause by clause, why each amount is what it is, and then the system keeps the amount and discards the reasoning.

The opportunity

Design the adjuster's work so that the reasons are a by-product of doing it, rather than a note someone has to remember to write. Then they exist to be carried back.

These are design findings from visible evidence, not validated research findings. Nobody has been observed failing at any of these tasks, and the page does not claim otherwise.

06 · Defining the Design Strategy

One commitment, and the seven principles it produces.

The commitment: a claim should carry its own reasoning. Every figure the system produces should arrive with the rule that produced it, at the moment it changes, in words a person can read. Most of what follows is that one idea applied to a different part of the journey.

Each principle below names the observation it answers, so it can be traced back to a screen rather than taken as a slogan.

01

Simplify the journey by removing decisions, not steps

Answers Three equally weighted routes whose correct answer depends on facts held elsewhere.

In the design The claim is staged in dependency order, so the system decides what can be worked on next and the person decides only what needs judgement. Four stages is more navigation than one worksheet; it is fewer choices.

02

Put the deciding fact next to the decision

Answers Network status sitting in a different navigation branch from the claim that depends on it.

In the design The hospital and its network status travel together in the claim header, so the fact that changes the arithmetic is never more than one read away.

03

Reduce effort by asking less, not by hiding more

Answers Seven required fields on an authenticated screen, including a mobile number and an email the account already holds.

In the design Nothing is collected that the record can supply. Progressive disclosure is not used to make a long form look short. The stage rail shows the whole shape of the work at all times.

04

A form should validate what it collects

Answers An admission and a discharge accepted on the same day, and an ailment typed as prose.

In the design Structure the field that decides the clause, and check a rule at the point it is decidable rather than passing it to adjudication. A claim that fails a known condition should fail where it was typed.

05

Policy terms belong on the line they govern

Answers A two-word cover description standing in for room eligibility, co-payment and sub-limits.

In the design Each coverage line and each settlement deduction names its clause, on the same row, in sentence form. Not a reason code, not a link to a PDF, but a reason that survives being read out loud months later.

06

Progress means naming what is holding the claim

Answers Four stages of silence, and a history screen that reports a stage rather than a blocker.

In the design Every state is specific: not in review but one implant sticker outstanding, and this is the line it is holding. A status is only useful if it implies an action.

07

Be predictable about what is settled and what is not

Answers An estimate typed by the member, and a settlement returned later, with nothing connecting the two.

In the design Confirmed figures and provisional figures are visually and verbally distinct. The settlement stage is titled an estimate, states its assumptions, quantifies what would change them, and says plainly that nothing on the screen authorises a payment.

What this strategy deliberately does not do is make the claim look simpler than it is. A health claim is genuinely complicated: a dozen documents, a policy with clauses that interact, and an outcome with money attached. The design does not hide that complexity behind a progress bar. It makes it inspectable, which is a different goal and a harder one.

07 · Information Architecture & Workflow

The claim stops being a destination and becomes the root.

The existing portal is organised by subject: policy here, claims there, hospitals somewhere else. That is a reasonable way to structure a product and a poor way to structure a claim, because a claim needs all three at once. The redesign inverts it. One claim is the root, and everything else hangs off it.

What changed in the navigation

Three things. Depth collapsed: reading a claim in progress today takes four levels from Home, and in the redesign the claim is the top level. The duplicate resolved: Network Hospitals appears twice in the existing tree, once as a global destination and once as a route card, and in neither place is it part of the claim, so the redesign makes network status an attribute of the claim's hospital instead of a place to visit.

Stages stopped being destinations: Intimation, Claim Submission and Claim History are three screens describing one claim at three points in its life. In the redesign they are states of a single object, and the rail shows which one it is in.

What the stage order encodes

The order is not presentational. Documents come first because a clause cannot be mapped to evidence that is not there. Mapping comes before coverage because a line decided without a clause is a line nobody can defend. Settlement comes last because it is arithmetic over decisions already made, and reordering it would mean producing a figure before the reasons for it exist.

That dependency is what makes the rail useful rather than decorative: a stage that cannot be completed tells you precisely which earlier stage is blocking it.

08 · Key Design Decisions

Six decisions, what each one gave up, and which two were not built.

Each decision is set out the same way: the challenge in the existing experience, what was decided and why, where the new experience can be seen, and what the change enables. The fourth of those is the one most case studies overclaim, so nothing in this section is stated as a measured improvement. None of it has been measured.

Two of the six are decisions I took and did not build. They say so in the place the redesigned screen would have gone, rather than being quietly left out.

Decision 01 Designed Not built

Simplifying the member dashboard, and deciding not to do it yet

The challenge

The dashboard summarises a policy as Hospitalisation Only with a balance of ₹4,00,000. Room rent eligibility, co-payment, sub-limits and the non-payable list are not on it. The obvious move is to put them there.

The design decision

Not to. Adding clause text to the member dashboard would show a member four more terms they cannot act on, at a moment when no claim exists for them to apply to. The terms only become meaningful attached to a figure they changed, and that figure is produced on the adjuster side, which currently discards the reasoning that produced it.

So the sequencing decision was: fix the side that generates the reasons first, then carry them back. Redesigning the dashboard before those reasons exist would move the opacity rather than remove it.

The new experience

There isn't one. This is the decision that scoped the project, and the reason every redesigned screen in this case study belongs to the adjuster.

No redesigned screen

The member dashboard was not redesigned in this project. It is named as a remaining opportunity in section 11, with the dependency that has to be satisfied first.

The impact

What this enables is a correct order of work rather than an outcome. The member-side improvement this project is aiming at, a settlement that arrives with its reasons, is only possible once those reasons are captured somewhere, which is what decisions 02 to 06 build.

Decision 02 Designed Built in the demo

Making policy and coverage information accessible

The challenge

A coverage decision is only as good as the reason behind it, and reasons are usually recorded somewhere other than the figure. The member feels this as a number that arrives with no account of itself; the adjuster feels it as a decision they have to reconstruct months later from memory.

The design decision

The clause sits on the row, in words, next to the amount it changed. The alternatives were a link to the policy document and a reason code; both were rejected, because a reason that requires a second lookup will not survive either the audit conversation or the member conversation.

The trade-off: denser rows, and a dependency on clause text being written in language a person can read, which is a content problem this design creates and does not solve.

The new experience

Stages 2 and 3 of the running workflow in section 09. Policy Mapping pairs each clause with the document that evidences it; Coverage Decision marks each bill line covered, capped or held and names the clause that decided it, including the room rent cap that also prorates the surgeon, theatre and anaesthetist charges.

The impact

Every deduction becomes attributable to a specific clause at the moment it is applied. That makes the decision reconstructable by someone who was not there, and it is the precondition for ever showing a member why their settlement differs from their estimate. Whether it changes how accurately adjusters explain decisions is untested.

Decision 03 Designed Built in the demo

Streamlining claim initiation by staging the work

The challenge

A claim carries documents, clauses, line items and arithmetic. Shown together they are unreadable; shown as separate destinations, the way the member portal splits Intimation, Submission and History, they lose their connection to each other.

The design decision

Four stages in dependency order: documents, then mapping, then coverage, then settlement. Each stage is a precondition for the next, so the order is not presentational. Mapping a clause to a document that is missing is wasted work, and deciding coverage before mapping produces a figure nobody can trace.

The trade-off: more navigation than a single worksheet. An experienced adjuster working a simple claim will feel the extra steps, and that is the first thing a usability session should check.

The new experience

The rail and all four stages in section 09. A stage that cannot be completed names the earlier stage blocking it, which is what makes the rail load-bearing rather than decorative.

The impact

The work can be picked up and put down. An adjuster returning to a half-finished claim can see which stage it is in and what is blocking it without re-reading the file, and a reviewer can follow the same path the decision took. No throughput claim is made; this is a structural change, not a measured one.

Decision 04 Designed Member search not built

Improving hospital search and selection

The challenge

The search returns registry identity, a Rohini number and an empanelment number, and withholds the relationship to the member's cover. Three results for one hospital name in one city, and no way to tell which one is in network, or whether any of them is.

The design decision

Show the relationship to this policy first, then the identifier. On the member side that reorders the result card. On the adjuster side it does something more structural: the hospital and its network status travel together in the claim header, because network status is an input to almost every coverage line and should not be a lookup.

The trade-off: this assumes network status for a given policy is resolvable at search time. If the panel data cannot answer that per policy, the result card can only show a weaker fact, and the decision changes.

The new experience

The claim header in section 09 carries the hospital and its status. The member-side result card was specified in section 07 as a flow, and not designed.

No redesigned screen

The member-side hospital search was not redesigned. The reordering above is an architecture decision; the interface that would express it does not exist.

The impact

On the adjuster side, a fact that currently requires a lookup becomes part of the claim's identity, so no coverage line is decided without it. On the member side this remains a recommendation, and it is listed as such in section 11.

Decision 05 Designed Built in the demo

Simplifying forms and data entry by grading what is missing

The challenge

Document requirements depend on the treatment (an implant needs a sticker and an invoice, a day procedure needs neither) and nothing surfaces that dependency at the point the claim is opened. Everything absent looks equally absent, which is the same failure as everything being equally required on the intimation form.

The design decision

Four document states rather than a tick: verified, needs a look, blocked, and not yet needed. The distinction that does the work is the last one. Bank details are absent on purpose, because they are needed to pay a claim and not to decide one, and marking them urgent would make the two genuine blockers harder to see.

Each problem document carries its specific reason: a receipt total short by a stated amount, and an implant sticker that clause 4.9 requires before the implant line can be assessed at all.

The new experience

Stage 1 of the running workflow in section 09. Eight expected documents, each with a state, with the two that are holding the claim distinguished from the three that are simply not due yet.

The impact

What is blocking the claim is legible in the first stage rather than discovered in the fourth, and a request to the member can name one specific document and the clause that needs it. Whether that reduces the back-and-forth that dominates claim cycle time is exactly the thing to measure, and it has not been measured.

Decision 06 Designed Built in the demo

Making claim progress easier to understand

The challenge

The sample claim has an implant line with no supporting sticker, and the settlement figure has to be produced anyway. The tidy options are to exclude the line silently or to assume it and flag later. A silently excluded line is the single most common way a settlement surprises a member.

The design decision

Hold it, visibly. The full billed amount stays on screen rather than being zeroed, the clause is named, and the settlement stage states that the estimate assumes the hold and quantifies what would change if the document arrived.

Paired with that, the settlement stage is titled Estimated Settlement, states its assumptions, and says plainly that nothing on the screen authorises a payment. A number this consequential should be hard to mistake for a decision.

The trade-off: a less tidy number, and an interface that admits incompleteness instead of hiding it.

The new experience

Stages 3 and 4 in section 09, where the held line keeps its billed amount and the total carries its caveat.

The impact

A hold becomes an action someone can take rather than a deduction someone has to explain afterwards. It also means the adjuster and any later reviewer can see what is at stake in the hold. The amount is still on the screen. No claim has been processed through this, so nothing is claimed about accuracy or dispute rates.

09 · The Redesigned Experience

Four stages, working, with a sample claim running through them.

The redesigned experience in this project is the adjuster's. It is below, built and interactive rather than shown as a picture: move through the stages from the rail or the continue action, select any document to see what it contributes, and ask the companion a question. 02

Policy Mapping

Each clause that applies to this claim, paired with the document that evidences it. Three states: matched, partly matched, and no evidence. The clause with no evidence behind it is the one that will stall the claim, so it is stated here rather than surfacing two stages later as an unexplained deduction.

The decision here is that mapping runs clause first rather than document first. A document with no clause is merely unused; a clause with no document is a hole in the decision.

03

Coverage Decision

A line per bill item with its billed amount and a state: covered, capped, or held. Every line names the clause behind it, including the room rent cap that also prorates the surgeon, theatre and anaesthetist charges, which is the deduction members are least likely to anticipate.

The decision here is to show the full billed amount on a held line rather than zeroing it. The adjuster needs to see what is at stake in the hold, and so does anyone reviewing the decision later.

04

Estimated Settlement

The arithmetic, line by line, from the billed total down through each deduction to a figure. Beside it, the sum insured remaining, because the two numbers are read together.

The decision here is the caveat. The screen states that this is an estimate rather than an approval, names the assumption it rests on, quantifies what would change if the missing document arrived, and says that nothing here authorises a payment. A number this consequential should be hard to mistake for a decision.

Every figure in the demo is sample data chosen to make the workflow legible. They are interface content, not outcomes, and nothing in this project has processed a real claim.

What the companion is allowed to say

The source mockup puts a Claim Companion in the third panel, labels it "Online & Ready" and seeds it with one message offering to answer questions about coverage, deductibles and policy terms. Nothing behind it is implemented. The design question is not whether to add a model. It is what an assistant on a claims screen should be permitted to assert.

The model routes. The code answers.

In the demo, answers are composed in application code from the same claim object the stages render from. Each one names the clause or document it came from. The result is an assistant that cannot contradict the screen beside it, because both read the same source.

That is a deliberate limit rather than a limitation of the prototype. A settlement figure produced by a language model is a figure nobody can audit, and on a claims screen that is the difference between a tool and a liability.

Where a model does belong is the step before: reading a free text question and deciding which of a fixed set of lookups answers it. That is classification, it is reviewable, and it never touches the arithmetic.

Proposed architecture

01 · The questionAsked in the adjuster's own words, beside the claim.
02 · Route itA model picks one of a fixed set of lookups. It does not answer. Designed
03 · Look it upAgainst the claim record and the policy clauses, under the adjuster's own permissions.
04 · Compose in codeThe answer is assembled from the result and cites its clause. Deterministic and testable.
05 · Or escalateIf no lookup fits, it says so and offers a person rather than guessing.

The model never sees a settlement figure and never writes one. In the demo, step 02 is replaced by a fixed question list, so even the routing is deterministic.

Safeguards the design assumes

  • Every answer cites the clause or document it rests on
  • An estimate is always named as an estimate, never as an entitlement
  • No answer is phrased as a coverage decision, because the assistant does not make one
  • A question with no matching lookup returns a route to a person
  • Health details reach the lookup, not the routing model

What is verified, and what is not

  • Captured The companion panel, its header and its seeded message exist in the mockup
  • Not implemented The mockup's input is not wired to anything
  • Not implemented No AI service, policy engine or claim data is connected anywhere in the source file
  • Designed The answering behaviour in the demo, and the architecture above

What would have to be tested before any of this is built

No validation has been run. This is the plan, written as a plan rather than dressed up as a result.

01

The question the study would answer

Can an adjuster reach a defensible coverage decision faster, and explain it afterwards more accurately, using a staged workflow than using a single worksheet? The second half matters more than the first: a faster decision nobody can defend is a worse outcome.

02

Method and tasks

Moderated task sessions with practising claims adjusters, using a seeded claim with known defects. The demo in section 09 is already close to usable for this, which is a reason to build a demo rather than a slide.

The tasks: identify what is blocking the claim; explain why the room rent deduction is what it is; state what the member would receive and why; say what would change if the missing document arrived; decide whether the claim can be settled today.

03

Measures

Task completion. Time to locate the blocking item. Accuracy of the explanation against the clause that actually applies. Whether participants treat the estimate as an estimate. Error rate when asked to recall the reason for a deduction unprompted.

04

What would falsify the design

If adjusters route around the stages to reach the figure, the dependency order is wrong. If they read the estimate as an approval, the caveat has failed and needs to be structural rather than textual. If the clause text on each row goes unread, the central decision of section 08 does not hold.

12 · Learnings & Reflection

What this project taught me about designing around a decision.

01

A healthcare workflow tool is a record of reasoning, not a sequence of screens

The useful output of a claims interface is not the settlement figure. It is the set of reasons behind it, which is what gets asked for weeks later by a member, an auditor or a regulator. Designing the stages so that reasons are a by-product of doing the work, rather than a note somebody has to remember to write, was the whole move.

02

Member needs and operational requirements were the same requirement here

I expected to be trading one against the other: give the member more explanation, cost the adjuster more time. It did not turn out that way. The clause on the row is what lets the adjuster defend the decision in an audit and what the member needs to understand their settlement. The place they genuinely conflict is speed: four stages is slower than one worksheet for a simple claim, and that is a real cost I have not measured.

03

The most useful sample claim is a broken one

The source mockup shows two verified documents and nothing else. That is the state a claim is in least often and the state a demo always chooses. Seeding the demo with a missing sticker and a receipt that does not reconcile surfaced four design problems the clean version could not have shown.

04

An assistant beside a decision has to be less capable, not more

The interesting constraint was not what the companion could answer. It was ruling out a class of answers entirely: anything that would constitute a coverage decision. Deriving answers in code rather than generating them is a smaller assistant and a defensible one, and on a screen where money is decided that trade is not close.

05

Designing from a product I use has a specific blind spot

Being a member of the portal gave me real evidence and one vantage point. It tells me nothing about a member filing their first claim under stress, or about an adjuster working forty of them in a day. The design rests on inference about the second group in particular, and that is the honest limit of this case study.

What I would validate, and what I would change next

First, whether the dependency order holds under real workload. If adjusters skip to stage four to get the number, the staging is wrong and I would collapse stages two and three into a single annotated worksheet rather than defend the model.

Second, whether the caveat on the settlement screen actually works. It is currently a paragraph, and paragraphs lose to large numbers. If participants read the estimate as an approval, the fix is structural. The figure itself has to be rendered differently from a confirmed one, not merely footnoted.

Third, the thing I would do differently from the start: design the member side and the adjuster side in the same pass. I split them for good reasons, and the split still produced a case study where half the argument has a working interface and the other half has a diagram.

Status

A design exploration with a working demo, and no implementation behind it.

The analysis is grounded in a live product. The workflow is designed and interactive. Nothing has been validated, built or shipped, and this page says so at every point where that distinction matters.
