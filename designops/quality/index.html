DesignOps / 02

Good design needs more than good judgement

A senior designer can look at a screen and tell you it is wrong. That instinct is real, and it does not scale. The moment a team is twelve people across five squads, “I don't think this is good enough” stops being feedback and starts being a problem — because there is no shared way to say why, and no way for the person on the other side to disagree with it.

What follows is the work of making that argument possible: a workshop to agree what good means here, an evaluation instrument that turns it into twenty questions with a scale behind them, a checklist for the everyday review, and a critique method for the conversation itself.

  • 1 completed evaluation, 20 scored questions
  • 4 versions of one instrument
  • 19 checklist items
  • 6 source files

02 — The tension

Quality is easy to talk about and hard to evaluate

The workshop's own reasoning, in its own order: design work requires quick decisions made by lots of different people all the time; small teams absorb that through proximity; and past a certain size the product stops feeling coherent because nobody is working from the same definition.

It also makes a sharper point, and this is the one I keep coming back to. Without clear benchmarks, design reviews become subject to whim and opinion. That is the actual failure mode. Not bad design — unarguable design.

The workshop sets out this reasoning as an argument. It does not document a specific incident, and nothing in these files measures the problem.

04 — Define

What makes a principle worth having

The workshop spends its first half arguing that most design principles are useless, then says why. “Delightful” and “simple” are universal — true of every product, therefore decisive for none. A principle earns its place when it could plausibly have been written differently.

The exercise

The workshop ends by handing the question back to the team. Everyone writes alone first, then the group looks for the patterns — which is the “grown from within” test made into a process.

  1. 1Everyone writes down the keywords they think best describe the organisation and its products
  2. 2First part done alone, then brainstormed as a group
  3. 3As a group, identify the common themes and patterns across the ideas
  4. 4Each group presents back to the whole workshop

Where this stops. These files contain the workshop that ran the discovery — the argument, the inspiration, the exercise and the team split into four groups. They do not contain a final, approved set of principles. I am not going to show you one, because I would have to invent it. What the artefact demonstrates is the method for getting there, not the output.

05 — Evaluate

Five factors, and what sits under them

The evaluation model splits a product experience into five factors, each defined by what it should achieve for a user rather than by what a designer should produce. Under each factor sit the parameters a reviewer actually inspects.

Discoverability

Allow user to quickly understand what the product offers and how to navigate through it

3 parameters

  1. Information ArchitectureWell-organized navigation menus help users locate different sections of the product easily
  2. Information DesignOrganize and present information effectively that help users understand the information and context easily.
  3. SearchabilityAllow direct access to specific item / content help users find what they need quickly and easily

Learnability

Minimize the time and effort required for users to become proficient in using the product

5 parameters

  1. ContentSimple text for labels, headings, CTAs make it easier for users to understand the context and instructions
  2. ConsistencyConsistent design elements and patterns throughout the product helps users learn quickly how it works.
  3. Visual DesignAesthetically pleasing visuals with clear visual cues and affordances help user understand the information and actions.
  4. IntuitivenessFamiliar design elements (navigation, gestures, patterns, flow, etc. that is widely recognized help users to interact effortlessly
  5. GuidanceOffers contextual help and tooltips that would guide users when they encounter errors and unfamiliar terms or actions.

Engagement

Captivate user attention and motivates them to explore and and assist in pursuing journey

4 parameters

  1. Visual DesignAttractive visuals and well-defined layout that captures users' attention and create a positive impression
  2. User FlowsGuide users through the product with intuitive flow, allowing them to complete tasks efficiently
  3. InteractionInteractive media (mico-interacations, gestures, touch, hover, sound, and etc.) that provide feedback, delight users, and enhance the overall user experience.
  4. ContentCompelling content that keeps the users engaged and encourages them to explore further - Product info, progressive disclosure, personalization, suggestions & feedback messages to motivates the user to pursue journey

Efficiency

Allow users to accomplish tasks quickly and with minimal efforts

4 parameters

  1. Information DesignMaking essential features or actions easily accessible with prominent entry point & CTAs
  2. ContentProvides straightforward and simple instructions that guide users through tasks efficiently.
  3. InteractionEfficient input methods like auto-populating fields, predictive search, drag & drop, templatization, and etc. help save users time and effort
  4. User FlowsUser flows that align with users' mental models, minimizing the number of steps required to complete tasks to save on time.

Error Prevention and Handling

Minimize errors occurrence and provide clear actionable error messages to resolve them

5 parameters

  1. User FlowsGuided user flows that lead users through complex processes step by step, reducing the likelihood of mistakes
  2. ContentClear & simple messages when user make mistakes or encounter error, indicating the exact problem and provide guidance (inline help / help module) to recover from errors
  3. GuidanceProviding context-sensitive help or prompts that assist users when they need it, without disrupting their workflow.
  4. VisualHighlight elements with errors using visual cues - color, icon etc.
  5. InteractionMicrointeractions / animation for instant feeback, confirming that their actions are being processed

Source: Framework.xlsx / Sheet1 (2). Each factor is defined by what it should achieve, then broken into the parameters a reviewer actually looks at.

08 — The everyday instrument

The checklist that runs before anyone else sees it

A five-factor evaluation is a considered exercise. It is not what you run on a Tuesday before handing a screen to engineering. This is the short form — seven stages, phrased as things to catch rather than qualities to admire.

01

I love my design, but only when?

  • Does every element on the screen deliver value?
  • Will the user achieve his goal with it?
  • Is it persona-checked?
  • What could make the design fail?
  • Does it meet UX metrics if any?

02

Error states provided

Bad error handling paired with useless error messages will make your visitors leave

  • Are all forms and input fields error-handled?
  • What does the error page look like?
  • Can user recover from the error?

03

Design system checked

Consistency is key, and DS is the single source of truth

  • Did you check if you reinvented elements that already exist in the design system?
  • Have you picked the right components as per the use case?
  • Did you introduce new font, icons, colors or shades of colors?

04

Visual feedback on user actions

When users click and nothing happens they will look for exit or click again.

  • Have you considered adding microinteractions?
  • Did you use animation while content in loading up?

05

Wording is awesome

Writing copy needs experience too

  • Do you speak the same language as your audience?
  • What do your users need to know on this screen?
  • Avoid jargons, and keep it short and simple that anybody can understand it easily

06

Ready for test / review?

Prototype that gives real experience

  • Did you cover all scenarios to test journeys?
  • Did I replaced all dummy data with real data and removed all duplication?

07

Developer handover prepared

Make sure your developers have an effortless experience

  • Make sure to write down every error state, blank state or animation placement.

09 — Discuss

Giving the conversation a shape

Criteria tell you what to look at. They do not stop a review becoming the most senior person's opinion delivered slowly. The last piece is the conversation itself — who speaks when, for how long, and what the creator is expected to walk out with.

Source & attribution

“Share & Critique your way to a Culture of Collaboration” is the work of Shalini Raghunathan, Co-Founder of 3 Big Things, dated February 8, 2025. It is not my material and I am not its author. It sits here because it is the method I would reach for, and because a page about design quality that quietly reused someone else's deck would fail its own argument.

5. Share & Critique for Collaboration.pdf, page 1

What makes this worth keeping is that it is specific where most feedback advice is not. It does not say “be constructive”. It says the collaborators write in silence first, for seven to ten minutes, and only then does anyone speak — which removes the advantage of whoever thinks fastest out loud.

And it ends with the creator writing down action items. Feedback that does not change what happens next is just commentary.

The choreography

  1. 1Creator12–15 minCreator shares work - 12-15 mins
  2. 2Collaborators3–5 minCollaborators ask for clarifications, if needed, briefly. 3-5 mins
  3. 3Collaborators7–10 minCollaborators write feedback and post to the gallery in silence - 7-10 minsIncludes time for folks to read and react to others' comments.
  4. 4Everyone15–20 minRound Robin feedback & conversation - 15-20 minsEach collaborator reads out their post-its (and explains briefly, if needed). Nominates the next collaborator.
  5. 5Creator5 minCreator records Action Items and schedules follow-ups - 5 mins

45-55 mins total, as the deck states it

Reconstructed from page 3. Durations and wording are the source's.

Custodian keeps time, facilitates the conversation, keeps it in scope. The stage timings sum to 42–55 minutes, so the deck's stated lower bound sits 3 minutes above the sum of its own minimums. The stated total is kept as written rather than quietly corrected.

Three roles, three C's

The method's substance is here: each role gets the same three qualities, and they mean something different for each one. “Concise” for a creator means naming what you do not want feedback on. For a custodian it means keeping time.

Three roles × three C's, verbatim from the deck
RoleContextualConciseCurious
Creatorpage 17
  • Share what the problem is and who are you solving it for.
  • Tell a story about your work's journey, inspirations and challenges.
  • Define scope. Where are you in the project and design lifecycle?
  • What aspects of the design work are you seeking feedback on?
  • What aspects of the design work do you NOT want feedback on?
  • Structure your narrative to precisely articulate why you are seeking feedback at this time.
  • Do not defend or solve. Feedback is a gift.
  • Listen to absorb and understand, not to respond.
  • Use "Why?" and "Yes, and..." to dig deeper and get the best out of the session.
  • Reflect and interpret. Translate feedback into action items that advance the work.
Collaboratorpage 20
  • Frame your feedback to focus on user's goals and objectives.
  • Stay within the scope of the feedback requested.
  • Critique the work, not the creator.
  • Provide feedback that is specific, objective and tangible.
  • Share your opinions with justification.
  • Offer insight, not solutions.
  • Highlight what's working well. Don't focus only on the negatives.
  • State your assumptions. Ask for clarifications. Probe.
  • Challenge to contribute, not to win. Disagree respectfully.

  • Ensure collaborators move through the 4 Players roles.

  • Diffuse tensions.
  • Ensure all voices are heard.

10 — The through-line

How the four artefacts sit together

A synthesis of the documented practices, not an operating model any of these files names. Each step is evidenced; the sequence connecting them is mine.

  1. Shared principles

    Establish what good means here, specifically enough to decide with.

    Workshop – Design Principles

  2. Evaluation criteria

    Five factors, their parameters, and a scale with a written meaning for every point.

    Framework.xlsx · DesignEvaluationMetrics.xlsx

  3. Review checklist

    The short form of those criteria, for the review that happens every week.

    UX Checklist.pptx

  4. Collaborative critique

    A shape for the conversation so the findings get discussed rather than announced.

    Share & Critique — third-party method

  5. Actions, and round again

    The creator leaves with written action items. Scores give the next review something to move.

    Choreography, stage 5

The thing I would defend about this set is that it works at four different speeds. The workshop is a once-a-year argument. The evaluation is a considered deep read. The checklist is a Tuesday. The critique is a standing conversation. Design quality systems usually fail by picking one of those and pretending it covers the others.
