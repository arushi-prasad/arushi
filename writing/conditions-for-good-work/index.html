Design leader building products, systems and teams.

conditions for good work

Eleven years in, the thing I am responsible for has changed shape twice. Both times the fix had the same shape — take a judgement that lived in my head and turn it into something other people could apply without me.

01 — Where this starts

The question changes before the job title does

When I joined Bajaj Finserv Health in 2019 the design function was two people. There was no process to speak of, and reviews were held together by goodwill. Someone would ask whether a screen looked okay, someone else would say they thought so, and we would all move on.

I fixed that the way most senior designers fix it: I looked at everything. If a screen was going out, I had seen it. That is a perfectly good system at two designers and a workable one at four. At ten it is the bottleneck, and by then the bottleneck has a name and it is yours.

What changed was not my title. It was the question I was being asked. For years the question had been is this good, and I was reasonably quick at answering it. The question underneath it had quietly become will this be good when I am not in the room, and I had no method for that at all. Everything I have built since — rituals, a ladder, an evaluation model, an allocation sheet, a design system — is an attempt to answer the second question instead of repeatedly answering the first.

This is what I have learned doing that, including the parts where the evidence is thinner than I would like.

02 — The years before

What craft is still for

I studied at MIT Institute of Design from 2010. My first real exposure to the work was an internship at TCS in 2014, on a visitor-management system — wireframes, prototypes, usability sessions, the ordinary grammar of the job. Then four years at Decos in the Netherlands, on enterprise software: ERP, document management, workflow automation. Five products, two of which I took from nothing to a shipped interface.

Enterprise software is an unglamorous place to learn, and it is a very good one. Nobody is using a document-management workflow because they enjoy it. There is no goodwill to borrow. If a screen makes someone do something twice, they notice immediately and they tell you. You learn to read a flow for where the friction actually is rather than where it looks worst.

That is the part of craft that survives into management, and it is not nostalgia. It is a speed advantage in a specific situation: when two people disagree and both have reasonable arguments, craft is what lets you tell which disagreement is worth the meeting.

A concrete version. When we specified Capsule, our design system, four platforms wanted four different button heights — twenty-four pixels inside a banner, forty on app and mobile web, forty-eight on desktop web. A manager reading a spreadsheet sees inconsistency and flattens it. A manager who has built those screens knows that a banner is a different physical target from a desktop form, and that the honest job is not to remove the difference but to record which differences were decided and which ones had simply drifted. The system documents all four. That decision took about a minute, and it took eleven years to be able to make it in a minute.

The same instinct showed up on Vytals in 2024, a healthcare ecosystem of seven products. Each product ended up with its own colour world — teal and navy for alcohol impact, amber and black for health intelligence, a dark mobile treatment for skin analysis. Written down flat, that reads as seven inconsistent products. In practice it was one grammar applied seven times, and the judgement about which layer had to stay fixed and which could move was a craft judgement before it was a management one.

None of this requires the manager to draw the screens. It requires the manager to be able to.

03 — Scale

The team becomes part of the product

On 17 August 2023 the design function was twenty-four people: fifteen in the UX competency and nine in UI, counted off the org chart in that month's DesignOps review. I held the UI competency — those nine — and ran design for one of the HRx squads. My title over that period moved from Lead UI Designer to Deputy Design Manager. A function of twenty-four grew around me and I was responsible for a named part of it. Those are two different sentences and I have learned to say the second one.

What actually changes at that size is less interesting than it sounds, and more demanding. The design work does not get harder. The distance between a decision and the person affected by it gets longer, and almost every management problem is a version of that.

Three things did most of the work.

Feedback had to stop being a personality trait. Early on, critique quality depended on who was in the room and what kind of week they had had. We ended up with six recurring rituals — weekly crits, fortnightly jams, a monthly share-and-tell, ad-hoc sessions anyone could call when stuck, quarterly deep dives, and a design lunch where work was theoretically banned. The useful one was the smallest: in a crit you can disagree with anything, but you have to say why. “I don't like this” is not a contribution. “This breaks the consistency we agreed on” is. That one rule did more for the quality of our arguments than any framework I have written.

Teaching had to be distributed. We ran more than twenty-four knowledge sessions, every one led by a designer rather than a manager, none of them mandatory. The topics were ordinary — motion basics, writing for designers, accessibility, component thinking, research synthesis, Figma variables. The outcomes were not: the accessibility session is why accessibility entered our design checklist, and the component-thinking session is why we restructured how design-system work was picked up. Making attendance optional was deliberate. A session nobody wants to attend is information about the session.

Growth had to be legible before it was requested. The design organisation ran on a career framework with five tracks — UX, UI, product design, design engineering and management — four levels from Foundation to Advanced, mapped across bands GB03 to GB08. I did not author that framework; it was operated across the design organisation and I worked inside it. What I did do was use it as the spine of every growth conversation, so that no development conversation started from a blank page and no promotion decision arrived as a surprise. Over that period twelve designers moved up a level internally rather than being replaced from outside, and three individual contributors became people leaders, which the team had not done before.

By October 2024 the organisation proposed a different shape altogether: designers embedded in a product squad for three to six months, reviewed quarterly, reporting into the squad with a dotted line back to the design team for mentoring, plus a few people held centrally for the design system, AI work and design excellence. A January 2025 roster shows twenty-one designers in seat against twenty-three planned, split between product-embedded designers, a central floating team, interns, and single owners for the system and the AI products.

I mention the roster because it is the honest picture of what growth looks like from the inside: not a line going up, but a shape being redrawn roughly once a year while the work continues.

In 2023 the design team won Best Team at the company's annual AOP awards, judged across every team in the business, and I was given the Stellar of the Year award in the same period. I can tell you what the team believed won it — that people across the organisation wanted to work with design — and I cannot tell you which initiative caused it, because nothing in my records establishes that.

04 — Standards

Quality as a shared instrument

The real problem with being the quality bar is not the workload. It is that your judgement is unreviewable. If good means what I think is good, nobody can argue with it, nobody can learn it, and nobody can apply it on a Tuesday when I am in another meeting.

So we built an evaluation model. Five dimensions — discoverability, learnability, engagement, efficiency, and error handling and guidance — each broken into named parameters, and each rated one to five. The part that matters is not the five; it is that every level has a written definition per dimension. A two on learnability is not a mood. It means users explore the interface before they understand how to complete the task, and there is a sentence on the sheet saying so. Two people rating the same screen are rating against the same words.

Underneath the dimensions sit the parameters that actually get fixed: information architecture, information design and searchability under discoverability; content, consistency, visual design, intuitiveness and guidance under learnability. That is the level at which feedback becomes actionable, because a designer can do something about searchability in a way they cannot do something about engagement.

Alongside it we kept a UX checklist — error states handled, design system checked, every element earning its place, the design checked against a persona rather than against taste — and a structured critique format. The critique method is not mine: it comes from a framework published by Shalini Raghunathan of 3 Big Things, with a timed choreography and a custodian who keeps the conversation in scope. I keep it in the toolkit because it works, and I name it because a governance practice that misattributes its own sources is not a governance practice.

Now the part that essays about quality frameworks usually skip.

There are three different claims available here, and only two of them are true for me. A framework can be created. It can be adopted. It can produce a measured improvement. I can show you the first: the model, the scale, the definitions. I can show you the second, partially: a completed evaluation exists with per-question ratings whose averages reconcile, and a separate recorded snapshot scoring a product at 3.5 on discoverability, 3.0 on learnability, and 2.0 each on engagement, efficiency and guidance. I cannot show you the third. No baseline was recorded before the model existed, so there is no honest before-and-after to publish, and I am not going to manufacture one by pairing two unrelated measurements.

There is a smaller, sharper version of the same gap. One of my sheets names four review layers — design team review, squad review, service, peer review — and records nothing against any of them. No criteria, no cadence, no results. That is a plan, not a practice, and the distance between those two is where most DesignOps work actually lives.

If I were starting this again, I would record the baseline first and build the framework second. Building the instrument is the enjoyable half, which is exactly why it is the half that gets done.

05 — Operations

Planning makes the trade-off visible

DesignOps gets a bad reputation because it is easy to do badly, and doing it badly looks like doing it. The test I use is simple: does this artefact help a designer make a decision, or does it only help a manager report one?

Our allocation sheet is one row per piece of work. Each row carries the story ID and title, the squad and sub-squad, the product manager asking, whether the work needs UX, UI or both, whether it is new design or an enhancement, a complexity rating of low, medium or high, a priority from P0 to P5, a start date, a target design delivery date, a target go-live sprint, the named UX designer, the named UI designer, and then five stage columns: discover, UX design, UI design, tech handoff, and pre-production review and fixes.

In the version I have in front of me there are seventy-seven rows and twenty-six different product managers. That second number is the whole argument for the sheet. Twenty-six people, each of whom is correct that their work matters, each of whom would reasonably describe it as P0. No amount of goodwill resolves that in a conversation. A sheet does, not because it decides anything, but because it puts the conflict in one place where a decision can be made on purpose rather than by whoever asked most recently.

Next to it sits a weekly availability grid covering April to December 2024 — one row per designer, one column per week, three states: available, partially available, on leave. It is a dull artefact and it is the one that prevents the most damage, because it converts “the team is busy” into something specific enough to argue with.

A detail I have grown fond of: the grid thins out towards the end of the year. The further out you look, the more blank it gets. That is not an incomplete document. It is the plan being honest about how far ahead anyone could actually see, and I would rather have a planning artefact that admits its horizon than one that fills twelve months with confident fiction.

The same discipline applies to goals. Our OKRs ran on five themes — design-system implementation, transformation of critical journeys, streamlining design processes and tools, delivery excellence, and building the team — with quarterly commitments under each. Two of those themes carried a “5X” label. I want to be precise about what that is: a target somebody wrote down at the start of a year, not a result anybody measured at the end of one. I have seen targets like that migrate into portfolios as achievements and I am not doing it here.

06 — Systems

A library is not a system

Capsule started from an ordinary problem. Three products, built by different teams at different times, each with its own idea of what a button was. Colour, spacing and type were being re-decided per screen, which meant the same control could look like two controls. Handoff was a screenshot and a conversation. A brand decision meant finding every instance by hand.

The countable part of the answer is easy to list. The blueprint specifies twenty-five components with anatomy, variants and rules, five atoms, four recurring patterns and four platforms. The documentation file carries seventy-nine documented component frames, each laid out as a real documentation page — anatomy, variants, behaviour, usage. Three products, one language.

  • 25Components specified
  • 79Documented frames
  • 4Platforms
  • 3Products, one language

And none of those numbers tell you whether the system worked.

The thing that actually changed how design and engineering talked to each other was smaller than the component count. It was naming the values. A hex code sitting in a comp is a suggestion; a named token is a contract, and you can hold a build to a contract. Once the values had names, arguments stopped being about taste and started being about whether the right token had been used, which is a question with an answer.

The second thing was writing the principles down. Five rules, written in plain language, that settle a disagreement before it needs a meeting — and, crucially, that can be quoted by someone who is not me. A principle only does its job when a mid-level designer can use it to overrule a senior one.

Here is where I will be honest about the limits. I can evidence what the system specifies. I cannot evidence what it changed. My own records have no documented contribution-and-review process and no adoption tracking, and I have no exported product screens showing Capsule components in a shipped interface. A frame existing in a documentation file is not the same as a product having adopted it, and the gap between those two is the part of design-systems work that everybody underestimates, including me.

That is the lesson I actually took from it. Launching a library is a project with an end date. Keeping one true is an operating commitment with none, and it is the part I would resource properly from the beginning next time rather than treating as the thing you do after launch.

07 — What is changing

The job is changing again

The most recent thing I have built is WorkPerks, a nought-to-one employee benefits platform that sits on top of an organisation's existing HR systems — flexible benefits, wellness, rewards, OPD, recognition, reimbursements. I led the product definition, the experience architecture, the interaction design and the quality bar. I also built the employee app, in React Native and TypeScript on Expo, using AI-assisted development.

What that produced is countable, so here it is: a hundred and sixty-seven TypeScript files, roughly thirty-six thousand lines, seventy-one routes, twenty-four screen files and twenty-eight shared UI component files. It is in pilot. It is not a product at scale, it has not been through the load a real rollout puts on a system, and I am not going to describe a pilot as a shipped product because the word sounds better.

The division of labour was clearer than I expected it to be. What stayed mine: product intent, experience architecture, interaction decisions, component behaviour, the visual language, prioritisation, acceptance criteria, and the judgement about what was good enough to keep. What AI carried: code generation, scaffolding, implementation iterations, debugging support, the repetitive engineering work, exploring technical approaches I would not have had time to try, and documentation. Nothing about that split moved a design decision onto the machine. It moved the distance between a design decision and a working screen from weeks to hours.

That shortening is the part with consequences for a design leader, and it is not mainly about speed. When the gap between an idea and something you can actually use is small enough, you stop arguing about what a flow would feel like and go and feel it. Several decisions on WorkPerks changed after I used the thing, and would not have changed on a prototype, because a prototype lets you skip the states where products usually fail — empty, loading, error, the second visit.

There is an uncomfortable version of this for managers, which I will state plainly. The career framework our organisation runs includes a design-engineering track, and at mid-level it expects a designer to be building components and prototypes with AI tools. That expectation was written before I had tested whether it was a reasonable thing to ask of someone. Building the app myself was partly how I found out — and the honest answer is that the tooling is good enough for it to be fair, and that the skill it actually demands is not coding. It is specifying. People who are precise about behaviour get useful output. People who are vague get confident nonsense very quickly.

I have also written a model for how a design organisation might operate with AI properly in the loop, across strategy, product thinking, experience, systems, build, quality and knowledge. It asks the questions I think are the real ones — what can be shared with these systems, how outputs get verified, how you stop generated experiences drifting from brand and accessibility, and who owns the final decision. It is a model, not an operating system anybody is running, and the site labels it a concept for that reason.

08 — Where this goes

What I want it to make possible

Looking back over all of it, the moves are more similar than they felt at the time. Rituals took feedback out of my personality. The ladder took growth out of my memory. The evaluation model took quality out of my taste. The allocation sheet took prioritisation out of whoever spoke to me last. Tokens took consistency out of individual discipline. Every one of them is the same operation: find a judgement that only exists in my head, and turn it into something other people can apply, argue with, and improve without me.

The risk in that sentence is obvious, and I have fallen into it. Turn every judgement into an artefact and you get a design organisation that is very well documented and slightly afraid to decide anything. The correction I use is to ask what a given artefact lets a designer do that they could not do before. Documents that only let me report upward get quietly dropped, and some of mine have been.

What I am actually trying to build is narrower than a philosophy. I want a designer on my team to be able to make a decision I would not have made, defend it in vocabulary we both already share, and turn out to be right. That requires a shared definition of good, a system that makes the cheap decisions for them, a plan honest enough to show what is being traded away, and enough room to disagree with me in public. When it works, my judgement stops being the ceiling.

The gap I would close first is measurement. I have built frameworks faster than I have built the evidence that they worked, and the honest consequence is this essay — which can show you what was designed and far less about what it changed. That is the next thing I want to be good at, and it is less fun than building the frameworks, which is exactly why it keeps not happening.

09 — Sources

Notes on the evidence

Every figure above is one of four things, and it is worth saying which.

01

Dated snapshots

Twenty-four designers is true on 17 August 2023, counted off an org chart. Twenty-one in seat against twenty-three planned is a January 2025 roster. Neither is a running total, and I do not publish a single headline growth number because four of my own records disagree about what it would be.

02

Recorded results

Twelve internal promotions, three individual contributors moving into management, more than twenty-four knowledge sessions, six rituals, five career tracks across bands GB03 to GB08, the AOP Best Team award and Stellar of the Year. Each is countable and traceable to one named file.

03

Targets, not outcomes

The “5X” OKR themes were goals set at the start of a year. Nothing in my records measures them at the end of one, so they appear here as what they are.

04

Deliberately absent

Attrition and average-tenure figures exist in my records but contradict each other, so they stay out until I reconcile them. The same applies to the design-system performance multipliers that appear elsewhere in my material with three different values. Individual personnel data — names, joining dates, grades, competency scores — is never published.
